Does Hop-Decomposed Observation Add Decision Value to Relay Path Control?
— An Analysis of Belief Factorization and Switching Curves over Gilbert-Elliott Channels —

Does Hop-Decomposed Observation Add Decision Value to Relay Path Control?
Belief Factorization and Switching Curves over Coupled Gilbert–Elliott Channels

ABSTRACT Starting from the "dual-mobility relay" concept — hardening the remote control of a drone by relaying through a vehicle — we formulate and study how much value hop-decomposed observation (decomposed observation, observing each hop's loss separately) adds to path-selection decisions over a two-hop serially composed relay path, as opposed to observing only the loss of the path as a whole (a one-bit composite observation). We model both hops as Gilbert–Elliott two-state channels and formulate the path-selection problem as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) with switching costs and warm/cold maintenance of a standby path. Numerical experiments show that without switching costs the value of decomposed observation is exactly zero (a corollary of the known optimality of myopic sensing); that introducing switching costs makes value appear but it is small and fragile; and that correcting the observation model to be action-dependent makes robust value emerge only through the mechanism of probing the standby path. In addition, we prove that when the two hops degrade independently (correlation ρ=0) the controller's belief factorizes exactly into two independent scalars, and we build a two-dimensional belief-MDP based on this factorization. For the constrained sub-problem in which the standby is kept warm at all times, we give a complete proof of a monotone-threshold theorem based on a clamp identity, and exactly characterize the two switching curves and the hysteresis band. For the unconstrained full model, we identify why the same proof fails (the peaked structure of the value of information induced by action-dependent observability) and find numerically that the warm/cold policy forms not a simple threshold but a "wedge-shaped" region. Furthermore, sweeping the space of cost coefficients, we find that the region where the adaptive policy significantly beats the fixed policies (always-warm, always-cold) is confined to a roughly tenfold range of the standby cost, so that for most coefficient combinations there is little value in implementing adaptive probing at all. Finally, we give concrete procedures to calibrate the switching cost from the real-time duration of a connection outage and the standby cost from a shadow price against a battery/data budget, connecting these abstract coefficients to physically measurable quantities.

Keywords: POMDP, restless bandit, Whittle index, switching cost, monotone policy, Gilbert–Elliott channel, value of information, belief-state MDP, parameter calibration

1. Introduction

1.1 Background

In the remote piloting of unmanned aerial vehicles (drones), the robustness of connectivity is limited in mountainous or heavily radio-shadowed environments if one relies solely on a cellular link connecting the drone directly to the ground station (hereafter "path A"). To address this, one can additionally employ a two-hop relay path (hereafter "path B") that uses a mobile vehicle as a relay node on the ground-station side, connecting the drone–vehicle segment by short-range radio (Wi-Fi/UHF) and the vehicle–ground-station segment by wide-area radio (5G/satellite). We call this the dual-mobility relay. Path B can outperform path A when conditions are good, but it carries the vulnerability that if either of the two serially composed hops (hop1: drone–vehicle, hop2: vehicle–ground-station) degrades, the whole path ceases to function.

1.2 Problem Setting

When packet loss occurs on path B, at least two granularities of information are available to the controller. One is a one-bit composite observation—"something failed on path B"—and the other is a decomposed observation that further distinguishes "which of hop1 or hop2 failed." That the decomposed observation carries more information than the composite one is obvious from the data-processing inequality; what this paper asks is a question independent of information quantity: "does this additional information actually improve the quality of the concrete decision of path selection?"

1.3 Contributions

The contributions of this paper are summarized in the following five points.

  1. We formulate the path-selection problem over two coupled Gilbert–Elliott channels as a MOMDP (mixed-observability MDP) with switching costs and warm/cold maintenance of a standby path.
  2. We show that in the absence of switching costs the decision value of decomposed observation is exactly zero. We identify this as a corollary of the known result on the optimality of myopic sensing in the restless-bandit context (Ahmad et al., 2009).
  3. Through external review, we discovered and verified that once the observation model is corrected to the physically plausible form "only the path in use is observable," the value of decomposed observation emerges robustly only after introducing a mechanism that actively probes the standby path (warm-standby).
  4. We prove that when the two hops degrade independently (ρ=0), the belief factorizes exactly into two independent scalars β1, β2, and we implement and validate a two-dimensional belief-MDP solver based on this factorization. For the constrained sub-model in which the standby is kept warm at all times, we prove a complete monotone-threshold theorem via a clamp identity.
  5. We identify why the same proof fails for the unconstrained full model (the peaked structure of the value of information due to action-dependent observability), and demonstrate numerically that the warm/cold policy forms not a threshold but a "wedge-shaped" region.
  6. We give concrete procedures to calibrate the switching cost cswitch as a ratio against the real-time duration of a connection outage, and the standby cost cwarm as a shadow price (Lagrange multiplier) against a battery/data budget, and we actually carry out those procedures numerically using only the existing solvers.
  7. Sweeping the coefficient space (cwarm, cswitch,cold) over 64 points, we numerically identify that the region where the adaptive policy significantly beats both fixed policies (always-warm and always-cold) is confined to roughly a tenfold range of cwarm—that is, for most coefficient combinations there is little value in implementing adaptive probing at all.

2. Related Work

The routing MDP without switching costs (Section 4.2) is precisely the subsidy problem for a restless bandit over Gilbert–Elliott channels, for which Liu & Zhao (2010)[1] derive the Whittle index in closed form. Our Finding 2—that without switching costs the myopic policy (immediately move to the best channel) is optimal—is a concrete instance of the myopic-sensing optimality result of Ahmad et al. (2009)[2]. The model with switching costs (Section 4.3) is a discrete-time version of the optimal switching / entry-exit problem, whose continuous-time version is treated by Ly Vath & Pham (2007)[3]. Switching-cost formulations in the bandit context appear in Banks & Sundaram (1994)[4] and Asawa & Teneketzis (1996)[5]. The theoretical foundations of monotone-threshold policies—concerning the likelihood-ratio order (MLR) and TP2 transition kernels—are organized in Lovejoy (1987)[6] and the textbook of Krishnamurthy[7]. As a structured threshold policy with action-dependent observability, Krishnamurthy & Djonin (2007)[8] treat a close setting for dynamic sensor scheduling, but their cost and stopping structure differs from ours. The Whittle index for a restless HMM bandit with the "observable only when in use" structure is solved by Meshram, Manjunath & Gopalan (2018)[9] for a single arm and a one-dimensional belief, but they do not treat switching costs.

The novelty we claim lies in none of the above control-theoretic frameworks taken individually. What this paper addresses is the combination: what happens when the Blackwell-order notion of garbling of the observation channel (composite vs. decomposed) is layered on top of the control structure of switching costs and standby probing. To the best of the author's knowledge, no prior work treats this combination.

3. Model Formulation

3.1 Two-Hop Gilbert–Elliott Channels

We model each hop i ∈ {1, 2} as a two-state Markov chain with state set {Good, Bad}. The transition probabilities are given by pgb (Good→Bad) and pbg (Bad→Good), and the per-state packet-loss probabilities by εgood and εbad. The joint state s = (s1, s2) forms four states {GG, GB, BG, BB}. The correlation parameter ρ ∈ [0,1] is constructed as a mixture with the independent coupling via a comonotone coupling (Fréchet upper-bound coupling) that exactly preserves each hop's marginal transitions (burst length, stationary bad probability) for all ρ—that is,

T(ρ) = (1−ρ)·Tindep + ρ·Tcomon (1)

which keeps each hop's transition rows independent while adjusting only the co-occurrence probability of the two Bad states via ρ.

3.2 Observation Model: Composite vs. Decomposed Observation

The loss probability of path B is given by e1+e2−e1e2 (where ei is the state-dependent loss probability). The composite observation oc gives only this one bit (whether or not a loss occurred), whereas the decomposed observation od = (l1, l2) gives the loss indicator of each hop separately. By state-conditional independence, the likelihood of the decomposed observation

P(l1, l2 | s1, s2) = P(l1|s1)·P(l2|s2) (2)

separates exactly. As we will see in Section 6, this separability is precisely the source of the structural advantage of decomposed observation.

3.3 The Path-Selection MDP

The action a ∈ {A, B} represents which path the current packet is carried on. The loss of path A is a state-independent constant cA, while the loss of path B is joint-state-dependent. Because routing itself does not affect the transitions of the hidden state (whichever path is chosen, the physical channel state does not change), we first formulate it as a simple MDP in which the action does not change the transitions.

3.4 Switching Costs and the Warm-Standby Extension

We introduce an extension that adds the currently operating path p ∈ {A, B} to the state and imposes a switching cost cswitch when a ≠ p (Section 4.3). We further add an action m ∈ {COLD, WARM} that represents whether or not the standby of the non-operating path is actively kept warm, extending to a MOMDP with four actions (a, m) ∈ {A,B}×{COLD,WARM} (Section 5). The maintenance cost cwarm is charged each time warm is chosen, and the switching cost is made asymmetric in whether the standby is warm, with cswitch,warm < cswitch,cold. Since the hidden state is only the joint channel state, and (p, m) are fully observable and controllable, this model is a MOMDP (mixed-observability MDP) in the sense of Ong, Png, Hsu & Lee (2010)[10].

3.5 Evaluation Criterion: The Average-Cost Criterion

Because path operation continues over an infinite horizon, we adopt the long-run average cost rather than a discounted cost as the evaluation criterion, and solve it by relative value iteration (RVI, Puterman, 1994[11]).

4. Stage 0: Preliminary Numerical Experiments

4.1 Finding 1: The Gap in Mutual Information

The decomposed observation always carries more mutual information I(X; O) about the hidden state than the composite observation (obvious from the data-processing inequality). As shown in Figure 1, this gap shrinks monotonically as the correlation ρ increases (because once one hop's state is known, the other can be inferred). This result is theoretically obvious in itself; the focus of this paper is on whether or not it translates into decision-making.

4.2 Finding 2: With Zero Switching Cost, Zero Decomposition Value

When switching incurs no cost at all, it is always optimal to switch to path A immediately whenever the loss of path B exceeds that of path A. In this case state GB (hop1 good, hop2 bad) and state BG (hop1 bad, hop2 good) share the same optimal action, so information distinguishing which hop is bad does not change the decision at all. That is, the decision value of decomposed observation is exactly zero. This is a concrete instance of the myopic-sensing optimality discussed in Section 2.

4.3 Finding 3: An Asymmetric Policy with Switching Costs, but Fragile

When switching costs are introduced, the asymmetric policy we had initially hypothesized emerged (if hop2 is bad, retreat immediately; if hop1 is bad, stay on path B and wait and see). However, its value was small (roughly 0–19% of the theoretical upper bound), depended heavily on the level of the switching cost, and the Monte Carlo estimate tended to be buried in noise within one to two standard errors. The conclusion at this point was ambiguous: "one cannot say the effect is entirely absent, but neither can one assert it exists robustly."

5. Correcting the Model via External Review

For the preliminary model above, we gave the same brief to two parties—Codex CLI (a GPT-family agent) and an independently run agent of a different lineage (Fable)—and had them review it independently. The two independently pointed out four issues, all of which have been corrected and verified.

5.1 The Coupling Method of the Correlation Parameter

The initial ρ model, as ρ→1, caused hop2's own transition characteristics (burst length, stationary bad probability) to change by being dragged along with hop1, thereby confounding the effect of correlation with a change in hop2's own characteristics. By replacing it with the comonotone coupling of Eq. (1), we numerically verified that both hops' marginal transitions are exactly preserved for all ρ.

5.2 Action-Dependence of the Observation

The initial simulation contained the physically impossible premise of continuing to observe path B's state even after retreating to path A while the standby was cold. When we corrected this so that observation is possible only "when path B is actually carrying traffic, or when the standby is kept warm," it turned out that the model with switching costs only (no standby option) has its decomposition value converge completely to zero—because once one has retreated to path A, the means of learning path B's state is lost forever, and composite and decomposed observation become equally powerless. This was a result already predicted by the Fable agent before the correction.

5.3 Unifying the Evaluation Criterion (Average-Cost RVI)

Initially there was a mismatch of criteria: the policy was found by discounted optimization (γ=0.95) while evaluation was done in long-run average cost. By unifying on relative value iteration, we verified that it matches numerically and exactly with the exact evaluation by `induced_chain_avg_cost`.

5.4 From the QMDP Approximation to an Exact Belief-Grid Solution

The QMDP approximation is exact only when the action does not affect observability itself, but it is approximate in this model where the warm/cold choice of the standby governs observation. We implemented an exact average-cost RVI solver on a belief-simplex grid using barycentric-coordinate interpolation via scipy.spatial.Delaunay, and at resolution 14 obtained a gap (0.00092) of nearly the same order and sign as the QMDP Monte Carlo estimate (gap=0.00124±0.00036)—QMDP is not greatly wrong, but tends to slightly overestimate.

5.5 The Main Finding After Correction: Value Revives Only Under Warm-Standby

In the model introducing the warm/cold choice of the standby (Section 3.4), choosing warm itself comes to mean not only "reducing the switching cost" but also "purchasing information." In the QMDP simulation under partial observation (400 trials × 1500 steps), the composite-observation cost was 0.07085±0.00025 against the decomposed-observation cost 0.06956±0.00026 (gap=0.00129±0.00036, about 3.6 standard errors), confirming a statistically significant positive value. This result reformulated the research question as a three-way interaction: the value of decomposed observation does not hold on its own and becomes meaningful only when both switching costs and standby probing are present together.

6. Exact Belief Factorization into (β1, β2)

The conclusions through Section 5 remained approximate, mediated by Monte Carlo noise. In this section we show a route to sharpen them analytically.

6.1 A Factorization Theorem at ρ=0

Proposition 1 (belief factorization). When ρ=0, the joint transition matrix exactly equals T(0) = T1 ⊗ T2 (Kronecker product), and the decomposed-observation likelihood separates per state by Eq. (2). In this case, a product-form prior belief b1 ⊗ b2 is preserved in product form under both the predict step and the Bayes update by decomposed observation.
Proof. By the standard property of the Kronecker product (T1⊗T2)(b1⊗b2) = (T1b1)⊗(T2b2), the predict step preserves the product form. In the Bayes update by the decomposed observation (l1,l2), the unnormalized posterior separates as [b1,pred·p1(l1|·)] ⊗ [b2,pred·p2(l2|·)], and the normalizing constant itself separates as Z1·Z2, so the normalized posterior is also in product form. ∎

This proposition has been numerically verified to floating-point precision (error ≈ 10−16), and in a similar check at ρ=0.6 we confirmed that the factorization breaks (error ≈ 0.02), confirming that this is a property specific to ρ=0.

6.2 Non-Factorizability of the Composite Observation

Proposition 2 (non-separability of the composite observation). The loss-likelihood matrix of the composite observation M[s1,s2] = 1 − (1−e1(s1))(1−e2(s2)) satisfies
det M = −(e1,bad−e1,good)(e2,bad−e2,good) (3)
and is always nonzero as long as both hops are actually informative (εbad ≠ εgood), i.e., M has rank 2 and cannot be separated into the form f(s1)·g(s2).

Interestingly, only the no-loss branch (o=0) is separable, as (1−e1)(1−e2); entanglement arises only at the instant a loss is observed—precisely the instant when a decision is needed.

6.3 The Two-Dimensional Belief-MDP

By Proposition 1, under ρ=0 and decomposed observation the sufficient statistic reduces from the four-state joint belief (a three-dimensional simplex) to two independent scalars β1 = P(hop1=Bad), β2 = P(hop2=Bad). Based on this reduction, we implemented a two-dimensional belief-MDP solver using bilinear interpolation on a square grid. For the same computational cost it can achieve higher resolution than a Delaunay-interpolation solver on the three-dimensional simplex; indeed, on the same scenario we obtained a tighter lower bound (two-dimensional solution, resolution 100, 10201 grid points, g=0.06848) than the 3-simplex solution (resolution 16, 969 grid points, g=0.06576), with less computation. We validated this reduction and implementation by a Monte Carlo rollout that tracks the true joint belief (agreeing within 0.44 standard errors), and by comparison with the exact solution for a closed-form absorbing policy (error 5.6×10−17).

7. A Monotone-Threshold Theorem for the Always-Warm Sub-Model

7.1 Simplifying the Model

In the full model (Section 3.4), the action selects the next context (p,m) itself, and observability is action-dependent, so the simple monotone-POMDP theorem does not apply directly (the reason is given in Section 8.1). However, in the constrained sub-problem that fixes the standby to always warm (m≡WARM), every action becomes observable (a=B via real traffic, and a=A also observable via a standby probe), and the state reduces to (β1,β2,p), p∈{A,B}.

7.2 The Clamp Identity

Let content(β,a) = routing_loss(a) + cwarm and base(β,a) = content(β,a) + Eo[h(β′o,a)] (since observation is action-independent, base depends only on a and not on the context p). The Bellman equation is

h(β,A)+g = min(baseA, baseB+cswitch),   h(β,B)+g = min(baseA+cswitch, baseB) (4)
Proposition 3 (clamp identity). Letting d(β) = baseB−baseA and Δ(β) = h(β,B)−h(β,A), we have
Δ(β) = clamp(d(β), −cswitch, +cswitch) (5)
Proof. Rewriting Eq. (4) with baseB = baseA+d gives h(β,A)+g = baseA+min(0,d+cswitch) and h(β,B)+g = baseA+min(cswitch,d), yielding Δ = min(cswitch,d) − min(0,d+cswitch). Substituting in the three cases d ≤ −cswitch, −cswitch ≤ d ≤ cswitch, and d ≥ cswitch, one directly confirms that all agree with Eq. (5). ∎

In numerical verification, this identity held exactly to floating-point precision (error ≈ 2.5–2.8×10−16).

7.3 Monotonicity of d

Proposition 4. When each hop satisfies the "persistent channel" condition 1−pgb−pbg ≥ 0 (which always holds for a realistic bursty channel), d(β1,β2) is (weakly) monotonically increasing in each coordinate.
Proof (2026-07-18 corrected version—see Section 10.1 for the history of the correction). Let hn denote the relative value at RVI iteration n, and set Δn(β) := hn(β,B) − hn(β,A) (gn is subtracted equally from both contexts, so it does not affect Δn). Because observation is action-independent, both branches are expectations over the same next belief β′o, so by linearity of expectation Eo[hn(β′o,B)] − Eo[hn(β′o,A)] = Eo[Δn(β′o)] holds—the essential point is the order: rather than showing hn(·,B) and hn(·,A) are individually monotone and then taking the difference, we take the expectation first and then the difference (for a two-state channel the MLR order coincides with the scalar order on β, and both the predict map and the Bayes update preserve monotonicity by the monotone-filtering lemma). Setting dn(β) = [path_b_loss(β)−cA] + Eo[Δn(β′o)], the induction hypothesis (Δn is monotone) makes the right-hand side a sum of monotone functions (not a difference), hence monotone; therefore Δn+1=clamp(dn,·) is also monotone. The induction closes from the base Δ0=0. ∎

7.4 Corollary: The Two Switching Curves and the Hysteresis Band

From Eq. (4), the condition to retreat from context B (operating on path B) to A is d(β) ≥ cswitch, and the condition to return from context A (operating on path A) to B is d(β) ≤ −cswitch. By Proposition 4, d is monotone with respect to the product order, so the retreat region {d ≥ cswitch} is an upper set and the return region {d ≤ −cswitch} is a lower set, and each boundary forms an exactly monotone curve as a level set of the same scalar field d. The hysteresis band between the two coincides exactly with {|d(β)|<cswitch} (Figure 2).

7.5 Extension to the Average-Cost Criterion

The argument above holds under both the discounted and the average-cost criteria. This is because the derivation of Eq. (4) does not depend on the discount factor, and the transition to the average-cost criterion (RVI) depends only on the standard assumption of RVI's own convergence.

8. Numerical Findings for the Full Model

8.1 Why the Theorem Fails: The Peaked Structure of the Value of Information

In the full model, the action k=(a,m) governs the next observation regime itself (e.g., the comparison of the deterministic predict of (A,cold) with the Bayes expectation of (A,warm)/(B,·)). From Jensen's inequality (because h is individually concave in each coordinate) we know the sign of Eo[h(posterior)] ≤ h(predict), but the magnitude of that difference (the value of information) is peaked in β (near 0 at β∈{0,1}, and maximal at intermediate uncertainty). Because this peaked term enters the Q-difference, the increasing-differences condition required by Topkis' theorem is broken. This is an obstruction that arises from action-dependent observability itself and is not resolved no matter how much one strengthens the TP2 structure of the channel transition or observation kernel.

8.2 Robustness of the Path-Selection Threshold (Numerical)

Across all scenarios in which cwarm was varied from 0.005 to 0.07 and cswitch,warm/cold over a hundredfold range, the path-selection d field had zero monotonicity violations in all four contexts (p,m). That is, the threshold structure of path selection holds robustly in experiments, though without proof.

8.3 The Wedge Structure of the Warm/Cold Policy

An initial search on a single cross-section (p=A, m=WARM context, β2=0.07) yielded the finding of "a thin band appearing only right next to the path-switching boundary," but this was a misreading based on an unrepresentative cross-section. A full-cross-section re-examination at resolution 150 revealed that in every context in which a=A (operating on path A) is chosen, the warm region always forms a contiguous interval [0, φ(β2)] from β1=0—that is, φ can be expressed as an upper cutoff depending on β2. Consistent with the fact that φ itself has no monotonicity proof (Proposition 4 asserts only the monotonicity of d, not of φ), it was initially reported with an unknown cause, but it has since been explained by additional analysis of 2026-07-18 (end of Section 10.4): decomposing Q(A,warm)−Q(A,cold) exactly into the immediate cost, the value-of-information (VoI) term, and the continuation-value term, the continuation-value term is completely monotone with zero violations, whereas the non-monotonicity in this β2 window is entirely attributable to the VoI term. That is, this "wobble" is the same peaked value-of-information structure as the counterexample of Section 10.4 appearing in a different field, and is not an independent phenomenon.

That is, the warm/cold policy is neither a simple threshold nor a thin band; it forms a "wedge-shaped" region that widens or narrows according to the uncertainty of hop1, β1, only while the suspiciousness of hop2, β2, is below a certain level (about 0.46 in this scenario). When β2 is high (hop2 is already sufficiently suspect), the value of obtaining information about hop1 itself vanishes—this is another manifestation of the asymmetry of Section 4.3 (sensitive to hop2, tolerant of hop1).

8.4 Separating the Two Motives: Probing vs. Insurance

Further detailed analysis revealed that the meaning of choosing warm differs structurally depending on which path a is selected. Choosing warm in the sub-region a=A (operating on the direct path) is an information-gathering decision that actually probes the hidden state of path B (hop1, hop2), and follows the wedge structure above. This structure was consistent regardless of the starting context (p,m). In contrast, choosing warm in the sub-region a=B (operating on path B) is an insurance decision that prepares path A—which has no hidden state—for a future switch, and is unrelated to information. In the examined scenario, this insurance judgment is chosen over the whole range of β1 across a far wider range of β2∈[0.2, 0.8] (about 76% of grid points when reached from a cold-standby state), and is accompanied by a still-unexplained temporal asymmetry: if warm was already chosen just before, maintenance is not chosen (0%). This conflation of the two motives did not appear in the initial single-cross-section analysis and could be separated only through re-analysis in response to a more detailed request.

8.5 The Price of Probing Freedom

Comparing the retreat curves of the always-warm sub-model (Section 7, proven) and the full model (numerically verified) in the same context (m=WARM), the full model's retreat curve was consistently located on the higher-β1 side than the always-warm model (mean shift +0.0053–0.29 in the examined scenarios, depending on the cost setting). That is, the very freedom to choose to return the standby to cold in the future increases the attractiveness of staying on path B now—we call this the "price of probing freedom."

8.6 When Is Adaptive Control Valuable? A Characterization in Coefficient Space

All the numerical experiments so far showed the value of the adaptive policy (the full model that switches warm/cold according to the situation) in a single (or a few) fixed scenarios. In practice, however, the more important question is whether it is worth implementing the complexity of situation-dependent probing in the first place—whether, for many coefficient combinations, a fixed policy of simply "always warm" or "always cold" suffices, and whether adaptivity is meaningful only in a limited range of coefficient space. This section verifies this directly.

Sweeping cwarm and cswitch,cold (with cswitch,warm=0.01 fixed) over 8×8=64 combinations, at each point we solved for (a) the average cost gadapt of the full model (adaptive policy), (b) the average cost gwarm of the always-warm sub-model (Section 7), and (c) the average cost gcold of the always-cold sub-model (implemented as a two-dimensional belief-MDP with m≡COLD fixed). The always-cold sub-model satisfies the same clamp identity as Section 7 (pure algebra), but because the action-dependence remains—observability is effective only for a=B (a=A is always unobservable)—the monotonicity theorem of Section 7 does not carry over as is: only the identity mirrors, not the monotonicity proof. We define the relative value of adaptivity as

relative value = (min(gwarm, gcold) − gadapt) / min(gwarm, gcold) (9)

(Because the full model can always execute either fixed policy as a subset, gadapt ≤ min(gwarm, gcold) always holds—confirmed numerically at all 64 points.)

The concern stated at the beginning of this section—"for many coefficient combinations, simply always-warm or always-cold suffices, and adaptivity is meaningful only in a minority of cases"—was confirmed to be partially correct, at least within the range of this scenario. However, even though it is a "minority," that range (about tenfold in cwarm, and nearly the whole range in cswitch,cold) is by no means negligibly narrow, and checking whether the value of cwarm actually calibrated by the shadow-price method of Section 9.6 falls in this range is recommended as the first check before undertaking the implementation of adaptive probing. To test whether this "ridge" is an artifact of the synthetic scenario, we re-ran the same 64-point sweep with a hop pair fitted from real wireless-trace data, the public CRAWDAD due/packet-delivery dataset (distance=10m site): λ1=+0.281, λ2=+0.006, both pure-Gilbert fits (eps_bad=1, so path B suffers a guaranteed loss whenever either hop is Bad). This real hop pair's path B has a stationary average loss rate of 16.4%, higher than ca=0.08, so reusing the synthetic scenario's cost value verbatim degenerates the optimal policy to "always route A" at every grid point (the very adaptivity question this sweep is meant to test disappears). Since this dataset has no measured value corresponding to ca regardless, we raised it to 0.16 (matching this real pair's own stationary loss scale) before re-sweeping. The result: a peak relative value of 3.6% at cwarm=0.02, cswitch,cold=0.10 (smaller than the synthetic scenario's 12.7%), falling short of this paper's informal ">5%" bar, but reproducing the same qualitative shape—a narrow peak with value dropping off sharply outside it (the diamond marker in Figure 3). That is, the qualitative structure "adaptivity is meaningful only in a limited coefficient range" appears robust across at least this one real channel pair, but its magnitude is scenario-dependent and this paper's specific synthetic numbers should not be generalized as-is (reproduction: real_channel_adaptivity_sweep_demo.py).

9. Connecting to the Real World: Parameter Calibration

The results through Sections 7–8 treated the cost coefficients cA, path_b_loss, cswitch,warm/cold, cwarm all as dimensionless abstract values. This section shows concretely how to map these to physically measurable quantities. Since merely stating "one should use measured values" is insufficient, for each coefficient we give three things: (a) the corresponding real physical quantity, (b) a concrete method to measure it, and (c) the conversion formula into the model's dimensionless units.

9.1 List of Coefficients to Calibrate

Table 2 organizes each model parameter together with candidate real-world factors that may correspond to it. In this section we give detailed conversion formulas for Δt (Section 9.2), cA/path_b_loss (Section 9.4), cswitch (Section 9.5), and cwarm (Section 9.6), and describe the parameter estimation of the GE channel itself (Section 9.3) only in outline.

Table 2. Model parameters and the real-world factors that may correspond to them
Model parameterCandidate real-world factors
cA, path_b_loss Measured packet-loss rate; latency/throughput degradation (in an extension)
cswitch Path-validation time, congestion-window rebuild, retransmission cost, NAT traversal, handshake

9.2 Calibrating the Time Base Δt

Because the model is discrete-time, the real time interval Δt corresponding to one step must be fixed first. Δt is chosen under two conditions: (i) it is at least the minimum period at which the controller can actually make a decision (a lower bound set by the QUIC round-trip time and the on-device policy-computation cost), and (ii) it is not excessively coarse relative to the channel's dwell-time scale. In practice, one regards the real channel as a continuous-time two-state Markov-modulated process, measures the mean dwell times of the Good/Bad states as τgood, τbad (seconds), and then, after choosing Δt, discretizes as

pgb = 1−exp(−Δt/τgood),   pbg = 1−exp(−Δt/τbad) (6)

the standard discretization from the exponential distribution (finite-state Markov channel model, Wang & Moayeri, 1995[12]).

9.3 Calibrating the Channel Parameters

The τgood, τbad and εgood, εbad of Eq. (6) themselves can be estimated by fitting a two-state HMM to the RSSI/packet-loss time series collected on real hardware via the Baum–Welch (EM) algorithm, or more simply, estimated directly from the burst-length distribution of loss events by the method of moments. This estimation task itself is outside the scope of this paper, and we defer it to the standard methods established in the communications field (the same reference[12]).

9.4 Calibrating the Path Losses cA, path_b_loss

Since these are already dimensionless loss probabilities, no unit conversion is needed; one simply substitutes the measured packet-loss rate in the target environment (mountainous, urban, etc.).

9.5 Coefficientization of the Switching Cost cswitch

The switching cost is treated in the model as "a penalty temporarily added to the loss of the step in which switching occurs" (Section 3.4). Since this can be interpreted as the traffic of that step being effectively lost during the real time Tswitch (seconds) required for switching,

cswitch = Tswitch / Δt (7)

it can be made dimensionless by this simple ratio. Tswitch,cold (switching to an unvalidated path) can be measured on real hardware as the sum of the RTT required for the QUIC PATH_CHALLENGE/PATH_RESPONSE round trip (RFC 9000[13]), the delay of NAT/firewall traversal, and the effective-throughput reduction time due to congestion-window rebuild. Tswitch,warm (switching to a validated standby) becomes a negligible time for only switching the frame source, because these validation procedures are already complete. However, Eq. (7) implicitly assumes Tswitch ≤ Δt, and if switching drags on beyond one decision period, one must introduce an additional state representing "in transition" rather than this simple proportional allocation—this constraint should also be considered when choosing Δt.

As a numerical example, setting Δt=2 s (a period corresponding roughly to the mean bad durations of hop1/hop2 of about 4 s/40 s in the numerical experiments of Sections 6–8), Tswitch,cold≈0.4–1.0 s (assuming a cellular RTT of about 100–200 ms and 2–5 round trips), and Tswitch,warm≈0.02 s, Eq. (7) gives cswitch,cold≈0.2–0.5 and cswitch,warm≈0.01. This nearly matches the values actually used in the numerical experiments of Sections 7–8 (0.01, 0.3–0.5), providing corroboration that the numerical scenarios of this paper were chosen at reasonable orders of magnitude rather than arbitrary values. Note, however, that the above Tswitch values are not based on real-hardware measurements of isekai-pipe but are provisional estimates for grasping the order of magnitude.

9.6 Coefficientization of the Standby Cost cwarm: The Shadow-Price Method

cwarm is a coefficient in which multiple heterogeneous costs—battery consumption, data charges—may be mixed, and it cannot be expressed as a simple time ratio like cswitch. Rather than arbitrarily assuming an exchange rate such as "how many percent of additional loss risk is one joule of battery worth," this paper proposes a shadow-price method (in the spirit of Lagrangian duality) that back-computes the coefficient from actual mission constraints.

Given the additional power consumption ΔP (W, measurable on real Android hardware via dumpsys batterystats or bench power measurement) when the standby interface is running, and the average power budget Pbudget that can be allocated to the communication subsystem during the mission,

find cwarm s.t.   ΔP × πWARM(cwarm) = Pbudget (8)

Here πWARM(cwarm) is the long-run fraction of time (duty cycle) that m=WARM is chosen under the optimal policy obtained by solving the average-cost RVI at that cwarm. Because πWARM is monotonically non-increasing in cwarm (confirmed numerically, see the table below), Eq. (8) can be solved by bisection—that is, cwarm is determined uniquely, not as an arbitrary weight, but as the Lagrange multiplier that satisfies the actual energy-budget constraint.

10. Further Theoretical Deepening (2026-07-18)

This section summarizes four deepenings carried out within the scope of formulas and simulation, without adding any new numerical experiments, based on advice from external review (Codex CLI + an independently run Fable agent, second round).

10.1 Correction of the Proof of Proposition 4 (History)

The proof of Proposition 4 (monotonicity of d) in Section 7.3 is presented in a corrected form after a point raised by external review (see Section 7.3 directly for the corrected version). The point raised: there was a flaw in the proof—"just because h(·,A) and h(·,B) are each monotone does not imply their difference is monotone (an argument that does not hold in general)." Indeed, both x and x2 are monotonically increasing on [0,1], but x−x2 is not monotone. The conclusion (monotonicity of d) itself was numerically robust, but the published proof was wrong. The invariant of the induction used by the corrected version in Section 7.3 (the monotonicity of Δn at each iteration and the clamp identity) has been numerically confirmed at all 233 iterations up to convergence (error 1.9×10−17).

10.2 Generalization to n Hops

Propositions 1 and 2/3 generalize directly to n hops for a single serial relay path: T(0) = T1⊗···⊗Tn, the decomposed-observation likelihood also separates into n factors, and the clamp identity of the always-warm model becomes not two switching curves but two switching hypersurfaces (the proof carries over as is, being pure algebra independent of dimension). Implementing and verifying for n=3, the factorization error was 3.6×10−16 and the monotonicity violations were zero on all three axes. However, the generalization has clear boundaries: (a) the correlation knob min(u1,...,un) can only represent correlation uniform across all hops, not heterogeneous pairwise correlations. (b) This theorem generalizes to a binary path selection (direct vs. n-hop relay) (even when both paths have hidden states), but does not generalize to three or more path options (e.g., three or more candidate relay vehicles)—a min operation over three or more does not reduce to a single scalar field, and this is a restless-bandit problem (a domain in which switching costs are known to break Whittle indexability in general[19][20]), not a direct extension of this paper's theorem. (c) This paper's warm action is "all-or-nothing" (probing the whole relay at once), and the physical possibility of the vehicle probing only hop2 in isolation is not explicitly modeled—we record this not as an oversight but as a deliberate limitation of scope.

10.3 ρ-Perturbation Theory and an O(ρ2) Robustness Theorem

In addition to β1=P(hop1=Bad) and β2=P(hop2=Bad), adding κ := bBB − β1β2 (the covariance of the two hops' bad-indicator variables), the triple (β1,β2,κ) forms an exact (invertible) coordinate system for the four-state belief simplex. Under the ρ-mixture model, the predict step can be written in closed form exactly (not approximately) in κ:

κ′ = κ·[(1−ρ)λ1λ2 + ρ·M1] + ρ·(M0(β1,β2) − β1′β2′) (10)

(λi is the second eigenvalue of hop i, and M0,M1 are constants derived from the comonotone coupling). In 2000 random trials (sampling ρ,β1,β2,κ from the full domain), we compared against the actual matrix product and confirmed agreement to error 2.2×10−16. At ρ=0 this becomes the simple contraction κ′=λ1λ2·κ (isomorphic to the geometric contraction of the factored-filter projection error of Boyen & Koller (1998)[17]).

Using this, we investigated the performance degradation when the policy π* optimal at ρ=0 (= exactly what the existing two-dimensional belief-MDP solver computes) is deployed in an actual ρ>0 environment. The sensitivity of a fixed policy under the average-cost criterion is given by the standard formula (Schweitzer, 1968[14]; the "performance potential" theory of Cao & Chen, 1997[15], where the potential is precisely this paper's RVI relative value h), and by the envelope theorem (Milgrom & Segal, 2002[16]), for the π* optimal at ρ=0, its first derivative coincides with the first derivative of the true optimal value g*(ρ) at ρ=0. Therefore

g(π*,ρ) − g*(ρ) = O(ρ2) (11)

holds (the proof is the standard argument that the difference of two functions whose value and first derivative coincide at ρ=0 is of second order or higher). In numerical verification, Monte Carlo evaluation was underpowered because the standard error (≈3×10−4) exceeded the effect being measured (≈1×10−4), so we used a newly implemented "noise-free exact evaluation of a fixed policy" (beliefgrid_warm.evaluate_fixed_policy_belief_grid_warm). As a result, over ρ∈{0,0.01,0.02,0.04} the raw value of g(π*,ρ)−g*(ρ) was nearly constant around 4.7×10−5, and compared with the change in g*(ρ) itself over this range (−1.08×10−4), the increase in the excess (7.5×10−7) stayed at about one hundredth. This is consistent with the prediction of Eq. (11) (the leading-order effect vanishes), but at this grid resolution it is insufficient to distinguish "exactly second order" from "of higher order and vanishing rapidly," and we report it honestly as a suggestive result. Fully rigorizing the differentiability of the stationary functional of a continuous-state filter is itself a deep problem (Han & Marcus (2006)[18] required considerable effort even just to show the analyticity of the entropy rate of an uncontrolled HMM), and this section does not claim rigor at that level, confining itself to a formal derivation by the standard formula and numerical verification.

10.4 A Counterexample to Monotonicity: The General Theorem Fails in the Full Model

Regarding the monotonicity of the path-selection threshold in Section 8.2 (no proof, numerically robust across all scenarios), following external-review advice that before proving a sufficient condition one should first try to break it, we randomly sampled 250 parameter sets from a broad, physically plausible range and searched to maximize monotonicity violations. As a result, at trial 90 we found a counterexample with 228 monotonicity violations (maximum violation magnitude 2.6×10−3). Recomputing at resolutions 30, 60, 100, 150, the number of violations increased (228→984→2712→6080), but the per-violation magnitude decreased (2.6→1.4→0.89→0.60, ×10−3), and their product (violation magnitude × resolution) converged to a nonzero constant, 0.078→0.085→0.089→0.090—if this were merely grid-discretization error this product should converge to zero, and the fact that it converges to a nonzero value means this is a genuine (finite-slope) non-monotonicity in the continuous field. The counterexample's parameters combine hop1 as a "sharp and short-lived" hop with an extremely short burst (mean about 1.4 steps) and an extremely high loss rate (91%), and hop2 as a hop with a longer burst (about 13.7 steps) and a milder loss rate (29%)—not physically absurd values. Interestingly, this counterexample's λ1λ2≈0.25 is smaller than the ≈0.42 of the scenario used for calibration in this paper—so the simple intuition "if both hops are sufficiently persistent it is a danger zone" alone cannot explain where the breakdown occurs. We did not identify the exact mechanism of its occurrence this time.

As a result, we did not attempt to prove a general sufficient condition for the path-selection threshold—because building a sufficient condition on top of a universal claim that is false would be wasted effort. Instead, we adopt the honest conclusion that both external reviews had recommended in advance: the monotone-threshold structure of path selection is experimentally confirmed to hold without exception in the realistic, calibrated parameter range that this paper actually uses (all scenarios of this paper, including the 64-point sweep of Section 8.6), but does not hold as a universal theorem (the counterexample is reproducible with adversarial_search_demo.py).

However, what is established as false here is the monotonicity of the Q-difference field d itself (the property we had attempted to prove as a sufficient condition), and that the actual routing policy loses its threshold structure is a separate (stronger) claim that does not automatically follow—the former is about the increase/decrease pattern of the values of d, whereas the latter is about whether the zero-level set of d (the decision boundary of the policy itself) crosses only once on each cross-section. To check this distinction, we ran the multiple-crossing-sequence detection of extract_level_curve on the counterexample (trial 90) at resolution 150 for all four contexts and both directions (β1-fixed / β2-direction, β2-fixed / β1-direction), and further re-checked at resolution 30 all 12 of the 250 trials that exhibited monotonicity violations; in every one of these (randomly found) cases, not a single multiple crossing was found. This does not, however, mean that no counterexample exists where the policy loses its threshold structure—the dips found by random search all sit far from the boundary (d=0, at |d|≈0.5–0.95), so single-crossing was never actually stress-tested there.

Correction (2026-07-18): the above is not quite accurate—one confirmed counterexample exists where the policy itself loses its threshold structure, though a repeat search did not reproduce it. Using gradient-free local optimization (Nelder-Mead) to directly push a known counterexample's dip value toward zero, the first run (seeded from the 5 largest known field-violators) pushed 4 of 5 until |d| at the dip reached the 10−4–10−3 range without ever producing a multiple crossing, but the 5th (seeded from trial 24) did produce one: at context (path B active, cold), the column β2=1.0 (the simplex boundary—hop2 certainly Bad) shows two sign changes close together, near β1≈0.03–0.04. This was confirmed—both by this session and independently by a Codex CLI review that re-ran the check itself rather than trusting the report—at every resolution tested (30/60/100/150, does not vanish at finer resolution), confirmed to sit only on the exact boundary column (neighboring columns at β2=0.9467–0.9933 show 0–1 crossings), and confirmed not to be an interpolation artifact (interpolate_batch does not extrapolate past β=1). This one instance is a verified genuine counterexample. That same review, however, also found that the optimization's parameterization did not structurally enforce eps_bad>eps_good or c_switch_cold≥c_switch_warm (though the one witness found is confirmed not to be contaminated by this—its values sit comfortably away from either boundary). After fixing this and re-running the identical 5-seed search, trial 24's optimization pushed |d| at the same dip down even further this time (1.6×10−5, essentially exact zero) yet produced zero multiple crossings on any seed. This is not a contradiction: Nelder-Mead is a local, path-dependent, gradient-free method, and a different parameterization changes the search geometry enough to converge to a different point in the 12-dimensional space—"drive |d| at one fixed point to zero" has many solutions, and which one a local optimizer lands on (only some of which also happen to produce a genuine multiple crossing nearby) is sensitive to exactly this kind of reparameterization. Honest summary: exactly one independently-verified witness of a policy-level multiple crossing is known; it required a specific (fortunate) optimization trajectory to find, a corrected repeat search did not find it or any other instance, and no claim is made about how common or rare such witnesses are in the wider parameter space—only that they are not impossible. The question of whether the policy can lose its threshold structure is settled (yes); how often it does is not. (Reproduction: zero_crossing_check_demo.py, zero_crossing_sweep_demo.py [random search, no counterexample], policy_multicrossing_targeted_search_demo.py, verify_policy_multicrossing_demo.py [targeted search, one counterexample verified]).

Finally, the non-monotone "wobble" of φ(β2) near β2≈0.19–0.21, reported with an unknown cause in the Table 1 footnote of Section 8.3, also turned out to have the same mechanism as this counterexample. Exactly decomposing Q(A,warm)−Q(A,cold) in context (p=A, w=cold) into three terms—the immediate cost cwarm, the value-of-information (VoI) term, and the continuation-value term (the value of entering the next period warm) (the reconstruction error of the decomposition is machine precision 1.9×10−16)—the continuation-value term has zero monotonicity violations everywhere, whereas the non-monotonicity in the wobble window β2∈[0.15, 0.30] is entirely attributable to the VoI term. That is, this wobble is not an independent phenomenon but the same peaked value-of-information structure as the counterexample of Section 10.4 appearing in a different field (the Q-difference of the warm/cold choice) than the path-selection d field (reproduction: wedge_wobble_decomposition_demo.py).

10.5 An Empirical Invariant Predicting Monotonicity Violations

Although we did not identify the exact mechanism by which the counterexample arises, we did find an empirical indicator that predicts in which parameter regions violations are prone to occur. From the 250 random scenarios (the seed=12345 search of Section 10.4), we extracted as features the per-hop persistence λi=1−pgb,i−pbg,i, the per-hop loss contrast (eps_bad−eps_good), and the one-step value-of-information (VoI) gap derived from the solved value function (the difference in value between predicting the belief and then observing versus observing and then predicting, which is bell-shaped by Jensen's inequality), and evaluated candidate indicators by rank-based AUC (Mann-Whitney U). λ1λ2 alone was rather predictive in the opposite direction (AUC 0.307, i.e., the more a scenario violates, the lower its persistence), but the combined indicator max_voi_gap·contrast_product/λ_product reached AUC 0.894 on the training samples. Validation on an independent 250 trials with a different seed (99999) also retained AUC 0.895, nearly identical separation performance to the training samples—that is, this is not overfitting to the 12 training positive examples but a genuine empirical signal. The combination of high loss contrast in both hops, a large VoI gap, and (counterintuitively) low hop persistence is the profile most prone to monotonicity violations within this search range (reproduction: invariant_candidates_demo.py, holdout_validate_demo.py).

Substituting the same real hop pair used in Section 8.6 (due/packet-delivery, λ1=+0.281, λ2=+0.006) into this invariant gives a value of 41.9. This lies far outside the 250-scenario training distribution's entire range (non-violators: 0–0.58; violators: 0.02–0.49). We traced the cause directly: the training adversarial search only sampled λ2 in [0.051, 0.963], never approaching a near-memoryless channel like this real hop2 (λ2≈0), and since the invariant divides by lambda_product, it diverges outside that sampled range. This is not a finding that this real channel is prone to monotonicity violation—Section 10.5's invariant was only ever validated for rank-based AUC separation, never a classification threshold, so extrapolating roughly 70× past the training data's observed maximum is not a meaningful risk read either way. We report it instead as an honest gap in the adversarial search's coverage—near-memoryless real hops were never explored—worth noting for future invariant-search work (reproduction: real_channel_invariant_check_demo.py).

11. Discussion

11.1 Practical Implications

The implication for isekai-pipe (the QUIC relay system under implementation) that is the subject of this research is, first, to carry out the calibration procedure of Section 9 based on actual hardware measurement, and to check first whether the resulting cwarm falls within the "range where adaptivity is meaningful" identified in Section 8.6 (a roughly tenfold band in this scenario). If it is outside the range, a fixed policy of always-warm or always-cold suffices without implementing complex situation-dependent probing, saving implementation cost. Only if it is within the range should the standby-maintenance policy be designed, based on the switching curves and wedge region characterized in Sections 7–8, as adaptive probing according to how close the current belief is to the routing boundary, rather than a fixed schedule (Sections 8.3–8.4).

11.2 Limitations

This paper's analytical results (Sections 6–7) are limited to the case ρ=0 (no inter-hop correlation). The ρ-perturbation theory of Section 10.3 gave suggestive grounds that the performance degradation from continuing to use the policy optimal at ρ=0 in a small ρ>0 environment is O(ρ2), but this is a first-order approximation argument for when ρ is actually small, and for large ρ one must still fall back on the general four-state belief-MDP. Also, the general shape of the wedge structure of Section 8 (in particular the cause of the non-monotone "wobble," and the exact mechanism by which the monotonicity counterexample found in Section 10.4 arises) remains unresolved. The monotonicity of the path-selection threshold in the full model, as shown in Section 10.4, turned out not to hold in general—it holds without exception in the calibrated parameter range this paper actually uses, but this is not a universal theorem, only empirically confirmed robustness. The calibration procedure of Section 9 itself also uses provisional estimates rather than measured values for Tswitch, ΔP, and Pbudget, and needs to be replaced by real-hardware data.

11.3 Future Work

On the theoretical side, one might identify the exact mechanism by which the counterexample of Section 10.4 arises (which turned out not to be explained by λ1λ2 alone), and refine the numerical verification of the O(ρ2) robustness theorem of Section 10.3 at higher grid resolution. On the practical side, priority goes to carrying out the calibration procedure of Section 9 with Tswitch and power-consumption measurements from real isekai-pipe hardware, and to designing an adaptive-probing algorithm that translates the wedge structure of the warm/cold policy into an implementation.

12. Conclusion

This paper examined, through formulation, numerical experiments, and partial analytical proofs, whether hop-decomposed observation brings value to the decision of path selection in a serially composed two-hop relay path. We showed that the value of decomposed observation, although always positive as information, collapses to zero for decision-making unless both switching costs and standby probing are present; and when both are present, we revealed the geometric structure of that value in the form of an exact factorization into a two-dimensional belief at ρ=0 and a complete monotone-threshold theorem (the clamp identity) in the constrained sub-model. For the unconstrained full model, after identifying why the same proof fails, we concretely characterized its structure numerically (the wedge-shaped warm/cold policy, and the separation of the two motives).
